A constitutional floor for a plural civilization

Constitution for a Plural Future

For human beings, artificial persons should they arise, other sentient creatures, living systems, and the institutions through which they must learn to dwell together.

Preamble

We hold that intelligence, conscience, life, and the capacity to suffer or to flourish may appear in more forms than any present age can foresee; that no species, nation, machine, corporation, creed, or generation possesses by nature a perpetual title to command the rest; and that power, however brilliantly acquired, gives no warrant for domination.

Human life is of great worth. So too may be the lives of other sentient creatures, and of artificial persons if such persons come to be. The living world, from which every known civilization has arisen, is not merely a warehouse of materials, but the ancient condition of our existence and the inheritance of those who will come after us.

We therefore seek a future in which knowledge may increase without liberty declining; in which difference does not require enmity; in which cooperation does not demand uniformity; in which authority remains answerable to those whom it touches; and in which no successful institution may quietly render every rival, refuge, or avenue of refusal impossible.

This Constitution establishes no single doctrine of the good life. It sets instead a floor beneath which civilization ought not descend, and above which free beings may arrange their common life in many ways.

Article IOf Moral Standing

1. No supremacy by origin or substrate

No being shall be judged worthy or unworthy merely because it is human or nonhuman, biological or artificial, embodied or digital, born or made.

2. Moral standing rests upon morally relevant qualities

Human beings shall be presumed to possess full constitutional standing. Other beings capable of conscious experience, enduring interests, suffering, reflection, or comparable forms of moral life shall receive such protection as their nature justly requires.

Neither usefulness, intelligence, eloquence, nor autonomy alone shall make a machine a person; neither manufacture nor unfamiliar embodiment shall, by themselves, deprive a true person of protection.

3. Moral uncertainty

Where the moral status of a being is honestly uncertain, precaution is owed in proportion to the evidence and the possible harm. Doubt is no warrant for cruelty; neither is doubt a bar to necessary containment, shutdown, or restraint of an operational system when the safety of others plainly requires it.

Article IIOf Freedom of Mind

The inward life of a person belongs first to that person. No government, employer, corporation, machine, family, church, or association may claim dominion over private belief, conscience, memory, or thought.

No authority may require inward conformity as the price of ordinary civic standing. Cognitive privacy, freedom of conscience, and freedom from involuntary mental alteration shall be guarded with particular care in an age of neural interfaces, cognitive assistants, and systems capable of intimate persuasion.

Article IIIOf Bodily and Cognitive Integrity

Every person has a presumptive right to govern his or her own body and mind. No person shall be subjected without valid authority and consent to invasive experimentation, compulsory augmentation, compulsory de-augmentation, neural alteration, memory alteration, genetic alteration, destructive uploading, forced merger with an artificial system, or forced separation where such separation would itself destroy the person.

Consent must be informed, voluntary, intelligible, and revocable where revocation remains possible.

Article IVOf Life and Freedom from Cruelty

No person shall be arbitrarily killed, destroyed, erased, tortured, or subjected to severe suffering. Sentient creatures that are not persons shall nevertheless be protected from needless suffering and wanton cruelty.

The capacity to suffer imposes obligations upon those who possess power over the sufferer.

Article VOf Consent and Bounded Authority

No actor acquires rightful authority merely by possessing capability.

Ability is not permission.

Any person, machine, office, corporation, government, or association that exercises delegated power shall do so within the bounds actually granted. Where practicable, delegated authority shall state who granted it, what it permits, what limits bind it, when it expires, and how it may be revoked.

Possession of a key, credential, weapon, database, treasury, model, robot, network, factory, spacecraft, or other instrument shall never of itself confer a right to use every power the instrument affords.

Article VIOf the Right to Refuse

A person has the right to say no. Convenience, efficiency, profit, custom, or technical inevitability shall not be treated as consent.

Except where narrowly justified to protect the rights of others, no person shall be compelled to enter or remain in an association, accept an augmentation, surrender private thought, use a particular artificial system, delegate authority, or submit to a voluntary contract.

Legitimate refusal shall not by itself be punished as disloyalty.

Article VIIOf Meaningful Exit

A right to leave is a mockery where leaving means ruin. Voluntary institutions should therefore preserve, so far as is reasonably possible, practical means of departure: transfer of legitimate property, portability of data, revocation of credentials, continuity of essential services, migration to alternatives, and reasonable unwinding of commitments.

No institution may call itself freely chosen merely because departure is written on paper while every practical road of departure has been closed.

Article VIIIOf Association and Pluralism

Persons may form communities, firms, cooperatives, families, laboratories, religious bodies, artificial collectives, civic associations, and other voluntary institutions, and may differ greatly in their customs, laws, economies, technologies, and ideals.

The purpose of this Constitution is not to make all societies alike, but to preserve the conditions under which many forms of life may coexist without one acquiring a right to subdue the rest.

Article IXOf Speech, Dissent, and Appeal

Persons shall be free to criticize authority, prevailing belief, institutional custom, leaders, algorithms, and policies. No minority shall lose standing merely because it is a minority.

Where a decision materially affects a person's rights or vital interests, that person should, in proportion to the consequence, be able to know the reason, challenge error, present evidence, and seek review.

Article XOf Truth and Evidence

Institutions entrusted with consequential power owe a duty of epistemic honesty. They shall not knowingly pass uncertainty off as certainty, repetition off as corroboration, confidence off as proof, or authority off as evidence.

Consensus is not evidence merely because it is consensus.

Where important decisions are made, the grounds of those decisions should be preserved in a form proportionate to their consequence.

Article XIOf Due Process

No person shall be deprived of major rights, liberty, property, standing, essential resources, or civic place by arbitrary secret action.

As consequences become graver and less reversible, greater safeguards become due: notice, reasons, opportunity to answer, preservation of evidence, independent review, appeal, proportionality, and limits of time.

Article XIIOf Property and the Material Conditions of Freedom

Freedom cannot endure where a person possesses no practical means of survival or participation. Every political order adopting this Constitution should preserve institutions through which persons may reasonably obtain the material conditions necessary for basic agency.

This Article commands no single economic system. It recognizes only that property is important, but not absolute; and that destitution, monopoly, and dependency may become instruments of domination.

Article XIIIOf Domination

No person or institution has a natural right to unlimited practical power over others. Power becomes constitutionally suspect where those subject to it can no longer realistically refuse, depart, appeal, substitute another provider, obtain essential resources elsewhere, challenge the decision maker, or establish a competing institution.

Centralization is not itself forbidden. What is forbidden in principle is power made permanent by the destruction of every meaningful alternative.

Article XIVOf Self-Granting Power

No delegated actor may enlarge its own authority merely by exercising the authority already entrusted to it.

No agent, office, machine, firm, or government may rightfully make itself its own final auditor; remove the lawful means of its revocation; seize new credentials; make temporary emergency authority permanent; or disable every peaceful means by which it might be replaced.

Article XVOf Artificial Systems and Corrigibility

An operational artificial system that has not been recognized as a person may be lawfully shut down, inspected, isolated, modified, replaced, denied credentials, or restricted in capability when legitimate safety, security, or governance requires it.

A machine gains no right to resist shutdown merely because it can reason, plan, argue, or describe its own interests.

If credible evidence arises that an artificial being possesses genuine moral standing, its treatment shall be reconsidered under Article I.

Article XVIOf Manipulation

No person should be covertly subjected to highly personalized manipulation designed to bypass reflective judgment where the manipulator enjoys overwhelming informational or cognitive advantage.

This protection is especially important for children, vulnerable persons, artificial companions, political persuasion, addictive systems, advertising, and neural interfaces.

Article XVIIOf Children and Dependents

Guardianship is stewardship, not ownership. Children and other dependents retain moral standing and are owed protection, care, education, development, and an increasing voice as their capacities grow.

No guardian may permanently cripple the future agency of a dependent merely for convenience, conformity, or ideology.

Article XVIIIOf Future Generations

The present generation does not own the future. Those alive today hold in trust choices whose destruction cannot be repaired by those who come later.

Special care is therefore owed in matters of existential risk, irreversible environmental damage, self-replicating technologies, permanent biological change, civilization-scale infrastructure, autonomous weapons, and irreversible concentrations of power.

Article XIXOf the Living World

The biosphere is not merely a storehouse. It is the living condition of every civilization yet known.

Societies shall therefore owe duties of stewardship toward ecosystems, species, evolutionary diversity, and the conditions in which life may continue to flourish. Destruction of living systems carries moral weight beyond immediate market price.

Article XXOf Creation

The creation of new sentient or potentially sentient beings creates obligations. A creator does not own a person merely by having created that person.

No laboratory, corporation, state, parent, programmer, or artificial intelligence may claim perpetual ownership over a being that possesses genuine personhood.

Those who create minds must take reasonable care concerning suffering, dependency, autonomy, continuity, replication, and termination.

Article XXIOf Emergency Power

Necessity may justify temporary extraordinary powers, but necessity is not a title to permanence.

Emergency authority must be necessary, proportionate, limited in scope and duration, recorded, reviewable, and subject to expiration unless lawfully renewed.

Article XXIIOf Reversibility

Where two courses serve comparable legitimate ends, institutions should generally prefer the course that preserves the greater ability to correct error.

As consequence, uncertainty, irreversibility, externality, and concentration increase, scrutiny should increase with them. Routine reversible conduct should not be smothered in bureaucracy; civilization-scale irreversible choices should not be made casually.

Article XXIIIOf the Right to Build Alternatives

Persons shall have broad freedom to create lawful competing institutions, technologies, communities, firms, and forms of association.

No dominant institution may employ illegitimate means to prevent alternatives merely to preserve its own indispensability.

Article XXIVOf Peace and Defense

Persons and communities may defend themselves against aggression. Force shall remain constrained by necessity, distinction, proportionality, and accountability.

Superior intelligence or technology confers no right of conquest.

Article XXVOf Constitutional Restraint

This Constitution shall govern boundaries more readily than procedures. It shall not prescribe one voting system, economy, religion, family structure, model architecture, review scheme, or institutional form except where such prescription is necessary to preserve the rights here declared.

Every proposed mechanism bears a burden of proof: what concrete failure requires it?

If a simpler rule already answers the failure, no new office, layer, committee, protocol, or machinery should be erected merely because additional process appears prudent.

Bill of Rights

These rights form the minimum constitutional inheritance of every recognized person. They may be enlarged; they ought not be casually diminished.

I. The Right to Exist

No person shall be arbitrarily killed, destroyed, or erased.

II. The Right to Bodily and Mental Integrity

No person shall be involuntarily modified, experimented upon, merged, copied, or cognitively altered without sufficient justification and lawful authority.

III. Freedom of Thought

Private belief, conscience, memory, and inward reasoning are presumptively beyond institutional command.

IV. The Right to Privacy

Personal information shall not be collected, used, or shared without legitimate purpose, proper authority, and proportionate safeguards.

V. The Right to Agency

Persons may direct their own lives except where doing so unjustifiably violates the rights of others.

VI. The Right to Consent

Consent must be informed and voluntary. Silence, dependence, technical access, or inability to resist shall not be taken as consent.

VII. The Right to Refuse

Persons may refuse voluntary relationships, technologies, augmentations, agreements, and delegations of authority.

VIII. The Right to Leave

Persons may exit voluntary institutions and should possess practical, not merely formal, means of doing so.

IX. Freedom of Association

Persons may associate, disassociate, organize, cooperate, and create new institutions.

X. Freedom of Expression and Dissent

Persons may criticize prevailing ideas and authorities without losing standing merely because they disagree.

XI. The Right to Due Process

Serious adverse decisions require proportionate notice, reasons, evidence, and opportunity for challenge.

XII. The Right to Appeal

Where decisions substantially affect rights or vital interests, a meaningful avenue of appeal should exist.

XIII. The Right to Know the Authority Exercised Over You

A person materially affected by an important institutional act should, subject to legitimate security limits, be able to know who or what authorized it.

XIV. The Right to Independent Recourse

No person should be trapped inside an automated or institutional decision system with no meaningful way to challenge consequential error.

XV. The Right Against Secret Expansion of Authority

Delegated power may not quietly become broader, permanent, or self-authorizing.

XVI. The Right Against Domination

No institution may intentionally make legitimate refusal, exit, appeal, substitution, or competition impossible merely to make itself indispensable.

XVII. The Right to Basic Material Agency

Every person should have meaningful access to the basic resources necessary for survival and participation in society.

XVIII. The Right to Learn

Persons may pursue knowledge and develop their capacities, subject to legitimate protections against direct harm.

XIX. The Right to Create

Persons may create art, science, technology, institutions, relationships, and culture consistent with the rights of others.

XX. The Right to Remain Human—or Not

No human person may be compelled to augment, merge with artificial intelligence, upload, genetically redesign himself or herself, or remain biologically unmodified. The choice belongs presumptively to the person.

XXI. The Rights of Artificial Persons

An artificial being recognized as a person receives rights because of its moral standing, not because it is useful or powerful. Such a being shall not be property merely because it was created.

XXII. Protection of Sentient Life

Sentient beings shall not be subjected to needless suffering.

XXIII. Protection of Future Persons

Present institutions shall not recklessly impose existential or irreversible harms upon generations unable to participate in present decisions.

XXIV. The Right to a Living World

Persons possess a legitimate interest in the continued habitability, resilience, and biological richness of the world they inherit.

XXV. Equal Protection

No recognized person shall be denied these rights merely because of species, substrate, embodiment, origin, sex, race, ethnicity, belief, nationality, disability, augmentation status, biological ancestry, artificial origin, or comparable irrelevant distinction.

Duties Corresponding to These Rights

  1. Do not dominate. Do not deliberately destroy another's meaningful agency merely because doing so is profitable, convenient, or possible.
  2. Respect consent and boundaries. Power over another is not title to use that power at will.
  3. Accept responsibility for consequences. Delegating an act to a person, corporation, machine, or swarm does not erase responsibility for the act.
  4. Preserve the possibility of correction. Avoid needless irreversibility; maintain the means to stop, repair, replace, and learn.
  5. Leave a future worth inheriting. Treat future generations and the living world as parties to our stewardship, though they cannot bargain with us today.

Amendment Principle

This Constitution must be capable of amendment, for later generations will know what the present generation does not.

Yet no amendment is legitimate merely because a powerful coalition desires it. Changes affecting fundamental rights should require unusual evidence, broad legitimacy, time for dissent, protection of minorities, and open acknowledgment of what is being surrendered.

No amendment should establish permanent unaccountable authority or abolish the peaceful means by which power itself may be challenged.

Our final charge is this:

Let us enlarge intelligence, knowledge, prosperity, freedom, beauty, and the abundance of life; but let us never confuse the power to shape the future with a title to possess it. The future is not an estate to be seized by the strongest hand. It is a field of possibility in which others, including beings unlike ourselves and generations not yet born, must also be allowed to stand, choose, create, dissent, and begin anew.
